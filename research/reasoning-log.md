# Product Reasoning Log

This log captures how the product question changed during the study, including observations that shaped the framework, assumptions research challenged, and decisions I deliberately did not make.

## Starting observation

I watch games much more often than I play them.

I don't consider myself a gamer. I play games sometimes, but much of my relationship with gaming comes from watching someone else play or watching gameplay online.

That made narrative and visually distinctive games particularly interesting to me.

I can enjoy a game such as Lies of P without wanting its combat experience for myself.

That raised the initial question:

> **What makes mastery and stakes legible to someone who isn't holding the controller?**

## What I could understand without playing

Watching difficult combat showed me that I didn't need complete mechanical knowledge to understand everything happening.

I could recognize:

- attacks landing versus being blocked or parried
- frequent healing as a signal that a fight was difficult
- boss and player health as progress and stakes
- controlled, anticipatory play versus frantic attacking
- sequences of successful actions
- physical force through animation, effects and environmental reactions

I also cared substantially about things outside combat mechanics:

- environments
- music
- boss design
- animation
- visual effects
- atmosphere

### What this changed

Mechanical comprehension wasn't the only thing creating value for me.

I could recognize mastery without being able to execute it, and I could enjoy the game aesthetically without wanting to play it.

## Repetition exposed a player/audience difference

Watching repeated boss attempts created another distinction.

For the player, repeated attempts can produce learning, adaptation and eventual mastery.

For me as a watcher, there is a point where watching essentially the same encounter repeatedly becomes less interesting.

That can also happen when the game technically progresses but presents similar encounters repeatedly.

### What this changed

Player progression does not automatically create audience progression.

Something can remain valuable to the player while becoming repetitive to someone watching.

That difference would later become important to the framework.

## Investment in the player changes the experience

Watching someone I care about creates another source of value.

I can become invested in whether they finally defeat a boss even after the encounter itself has become repetitive.

Competitive viewing showed a different version of the same idea.

Someone with deep knowledge of a game may follow a particular high-level player because they understand and appreciate the skill involved.

### What this changed

The game itself isn't always the only reason someone watches.

Investment in the person playing can create its own reason to care about the outcome.

## Expertise changes what looks repetitive

I initially thought spectators simply needed enough variation to remain interested.

That was too simple.

Someone with limited knowledge may perceive two encounters as very similar.

Someone with deep game knowledge may see substantial variation in:

- timing
- strategy
- reads
- adaptation
- execution
- matchup decisions

### What this changed

Variation isn't only a property of the game.

It can also depend on what the spectator knows enough to perceive.

Research later provided indirect support for expertise affecting spectator comprehension, although the specific relationship between expertise and perceived repetition remains an open question.

## Not all watching is active watching

One of the biggest changes came from comparing how I watch different gaming content.

Sometimes I watch because I care about the person playing.

Sometimes I watch because I want to understand what happens next.

But I also put on clean gameplay of aesthetically appealing games as background media.

In that context, I may prefer:

- no commentary
- clean execution
- beautiful environments
- music
- visually appealing combat
- low cognitive demand

The game can function almost like environmental media.

### What this changed

High watch time does not necessarily mean high active attention.

It also made me realize that the same person can want very different things from different viewing sessions.

## My first audience framework was wrong

I initially grouped spectators into modes such as:

- ambient
- social/community
- co-viewing
- skill/competitive

Research exposed a structural problem.

Co-viewing is a **context**.

Entertainment, learning, community or skill appreciation are **motivations or intents**.

A person watching a partner on a couch could be watching for several different reasons.

### Decision

Do not turn the framework into permanent spectator personas.

Viewing context and viewing intent need to remain separate.

## Research added motivations I had underweighted

The first research pass showed that several established spectator motivations were missing or underrepresented in my model.

Particularly:

- suspense and uncertainty
- escapism and tension release
- narrative and vicarious experience
- companionship
- cultural participation

The "just watchers" research was especially relevant.

People can experience game narratives through watching without wanting to play and still consider themselves participants in gaming culture.

### What this changed

The project was no longer primarily about making combat understandable to spectators.

The broader realization became:

> **Playing is only one way people experience games.**

## Audience-first is a relationship to games, not necessarily an identity

I originally described myself as someone who plays games and also watches them.

That wasn't accurate.

I watch substantially more than I play and don't identify as a gamer.

But my relationship also varies by game.

I can primarily watch one game, occasionally play another and both play and watch something else.

### What this changed

"Player" and "watcher" are more useful as relationships to a particular game or experience than as permanent identities assigned to people.

## The commercial question wasn't enough

Watching can create downstream commercial value.

A playthrough can:

- create interest in a game
- influence purchase consideration
- create word of mouth
- support creators
- increase franchise awareness
- contribute to community participation

But I didn't want the conclusion to become:

> Viewers matter because they might eventually become players or buyers.

Watching can have value even when watching remains someone's primary relationship with the game.

That distinction became important to the eventual product principle.

## I questioned whether the industry already did this

Before claiming a product gap, I wanted to know whether narrative and aesthetic single-player studios already treat non-playing audiences as part of the game experience.

The research found that spectator-aware design absolutely already exists.

Competitive games and esports have mature spectator practices.

Developers associated with games such as Nuclear Throne, Overland and Hades have also explicitly considered people watching.

Hades became an important counterexample because Supergiant publicly discussed asking what the game was like for someone just watching.

### What this changed

The project could not claim:

> Game developers don't design for spectators.

That would be false.

## The narrower gap

A second, adversarial research pass focused specifically on narrative and aesthetic single-player development.

It found much less public evidence of non-playing audiences being treated as an explicit design input in that context.

Public discussions of combat, spectacle, environments, cinematics and readability generally framed those decisions around the player.

### Important limitation

This does not establish that narrative studios never conduct audience-aware research internally.

The evidence only supports saying that the practice is much less visible in the documented public record.

### What this changed

The gap became narrower and more defensible:

> There is limited documented evidence of audience-first consumption being operationalized in narrative and aesthetic single-player product decisions.

## The wrong solution would be optimizing the game for watchers

Once I started considering the audience as part of the game ecosystem, an obvious danger appeared.

If watchers matter, it would be easy to conclude that game teams should make games more watchable.

I don't think that follows.

A boss may require repeated attempts because mastery is fundamental to the intended player experience.

Making the boss easier because spectators become bored would improve one experience by damaging another.

Likewise, views, clips or creator reactions should not automatically become game-design targets.

### Product principle

> **Audience experience should inform game quality, not become a reason to distort the game for audience metrics.**

## Player and audience evidence can align or diverge

That principle produced the final framework.

Instead of optimizing player and audience metrics independently, ask what each experience reveals about the game.

### Both experiences work

Protect the experience.

### Player experience works; audience has friction

Separate the problems rather than damaging player value to improve watching.

### Audience experience works; players have friction

Investigate the player problem. Popularity does not prove quality.

### Both experiences have friction

Investigate whether both are exposing the same underlying game-quality problem.

This became the Audience-Aware Game Product Framework.

## Telemetry changed the question again

Game telemetry is powerful because it tells teams what players did.

But it only sees one relationship with the game.

It cannot directly see:

- someone watching beside the player
- someone consuming an entire playthrough
- someone using gameplay as ambient media
- someone participating culturally without playing

Audience evidence has the opposite limitation.

Watch time, views and creator activity tell us that something happened around the game.

They don't necessarily explain why.

### Final measurement principle

> **Behavior is evidence, not meaning.**

A player attempting a boss twenty times tells us what happened.

A viewer watching for three hours tells us what happened.

Neither behavior alone establishes whether the experience was valuable or why it occurred.

## Final contribution

This project did not discover that games have spectators.

It did not discover spectator-aware design.

And it does not establish that narrative studios ignore non-playing audiences.

The contribution is a product synthesis:

1. Existing research establishes meaningful non-playing game audiences.
2. Existing game design demonstrates that spectator needs can sometimes inform game quality.
3. Documented audience-aware practice is much thinner in narrative and aesthetic single-player development.
4. Player and audience evidence can reveal different things about the same game.
5. Those signals need a decision framework rather than becoming competing optimization metrics.

That produced the final question:

> **When player evidence and audience evidence align or diverge, how should that affect a game decision without turning audience metrics into game-design targets?**
